# Working Thesis: MCP, Agents and the Machine-Mediated Commerce Frontier

## Thesis in one sentence

**Digital commerce is moving from human-operated interfaces toward machine-readable, agent-mediated markets in which AI agents increasingly interpret intent, discover counterparties, access enterprise capabilities, make or recommend decisions, coordinate transactions, and manage outcomes; MCP will be an important interoperability layer in this transition, but sustainable value will accrue primarily to the organisations that control trusted intent, differentiated data, executable capabilities, delegated authority, transaction orchestration, settlement, and ecosystem access.**

## Core argument

The next digital-commerce front is broader than conversational shopping or AI-enabled websites. It is the emergence of **machine-mediated economic interaction**: exchanges in which software agents participate directly in forming requirements, locating supply, assessing alternatives, invoking services, negotiating terms, securing approval, executing transactions, and managing fulfilment or service outcomes.

This shift should be analysed from the broadest level inward:

1. **Machine-mediated economic interaction** — autonomous or semi-autonomous exchanges between agents, machines, organisations, and digital services.
2. **Enterprise commerce** — procurement, sales, service, supply chain, finance, partner, and intercompany processes increasingly executed through agents.
3. **Customer commerce** — consumer discovery, comparison, recommendation, purchase, payment, fulfilment, support, renewal, and returns mediated through personal or platform agents.

The central strategic change is not simply that AI improves existing commerce channels. It is that the **front door to demand and the interface to enterprise capabilities may move away from websites, applications, search results, call centres, and manually operated workflows toward agents and machine-readable services**.

MCP can lower the cost of making tools, data, and enterprise actions accessible to AI applications. However, MCP does not independently solve agent discovery, agent-to-agent coordination, commerce semantics, delegated purchasing authority, contracting, identity, payment, settlement, liability, fulfilment, or assurance. Agentic commerce will therefore depend on a layered architecture of protocols, APIs, event systems, identity and authority mechanisms, commerce standards, transaction rails, evaluation systems, and governance controls.

## Eight propositions to test

### 1. The commerce interface will shift from presentation to intent

Traditional digital commerce optimises pages, applications, funnels, search rankings, advertisements, and human navigation. Agent-mediated commerce will increasingly optimise for structured intent, machine-readable capabilities, verifiable product and service data, policy-compliant actions, and measurable outcomes.

The strategic question will move from **“How do we attract a human to our interface?”** toward **“How does a trusted agent discover, understand, select, and transact with our capabilities?”**

### 2. Enterprise capabilities will become products for both humans and machines

Organisations will need to expose selected data, decisions, workflows, and transactions as governed capabilities that agents can discover and invoke. This will require more than adding an AI interface to legacy systems. It will require capability design, semantic data, permissions, service contracts, observability, and lifecycle management.

Companies with modular, well-governed and machine-accessible capabilities should be able to participate in more agent ecosystems than companies dependent on tightly coupled processes and opaque legacy interfaces.

### 3. The protocol environment will remain layered

No single protocol is likely to cover the complete agentic-commerce lifecycle.

MCP and comparable mechanisms will address access to tools and context. Agent-coordination protocols will support delegation and collaboration. Commerce protocols will describe products, offers, carts, orders, and post-purchase actions. Payment and authority protocols will address mandates, credentials, limits, proof, and settlement. Existing APIs, event architectures, industry standards, and systems of record will remain essential.

The strategic issue is therefore not whether one protocol “wins,” but which protocols become dominant at each layer, how they interoperate, and where control and value concentrate.

### 4. Bounded machine and enterprise contexts may mature before open autonomy

The broad transition begins with machine-mediated interaction, but autonomy will not develop uniformly. Bounded enterprise processes with defined counterparties, policies, budgets, data, and controls may support higher levels of autonomy sooner than unrestricted consumer or cross-market transactions.

The research should determine where this proposition holds and where consumer-facing platforms, payment networks, or marketplaces are accelerating faster.

### 5. Trust will become both a constraint and a strategic control point

As agents move from recommending to executing, the critical bottlenecks become identity, delegated authority, consent, policy enforcement, data rights, security, transaction evidence, dispute handling, liability, and reversibility.

Trust infrastructure will not merely reduce risk. It may become a source of distribution, market access, differentiation, and value capture. Organisations recognised as trusted agents, trusted merchants, trusted capability providers, or trusted transaction intermediaries may obtain privileged ecosystem positions.

### 6. Value will migrate, but not automatically to model providers

Connectivity and basic agent construction may become increasingly standardised. Durable value is more likely to concentrate around scarce control points, including:

* ownership or interpretation of customer and enterprise intent;
* proprietary domain data and knowledge;
* differentiated executable capabilities;
* identity, authority, credentials, and policy enforcement;
* transaction and payment orchestration;
* trusted distribution and ecosystem access;
* workflow embedding and switching costs;
* assurance, evaluation, monitoring, and accountability;
* proprietary outcome data and continuous-learning loops.

The research must test which of these control points are defensible and which will be commoditised.

### 7. Maturity will be uneven and cannot be represented by one score

An organisation may possess strong AI engineering while lacking machine-readable product data, transaction authority, operating controls, partner readiness, or a viable business model. It may also have strong foundations but no use case capable of producing material value.

Assessment must therefore distinguish:

1. **Foundational maturity** — whether the organisation has the capabilities required to participate safely and reliably.
2. **Use-case readiness** — whether a specific opportunity is valuable, deliverable, governable, and supported by the external ecosystem.
3. **Value realisation** — whether deployed agents generate adoption, revenue, margin, productivity, resilience, service quality, or strategic advantage.

### 8. The consulting opportunity extends beyond agent implementation

The consulting opportunity will shift from isolated AI pilots toward a connected portfolio of services:

* agentic-commerce strategy and market positioning;
* enterprise and ecosystem maturity diagnostics;
* use-case and investment portfolio design;
* agent-ready data and enterprise architecture;
* capability productisation and protocol integration;
* identity, authority, trust, security, and governance;
* agent evaluation, observability, and assurance;
* sector-specific transformation programmes;
* partner and platform strategy;
* managed agent operations and continuous value optimisation.

A consulting firm that develops reusable diagnostics, sector benchmarks, technical accelerators, control frameworks, and ecosystem partnerships may create more durable differentiation than one focused primarily on agent development capacity.

## Three-horizon interpretation

### Horizon 1: 0–18 months

The emphasis is on readiness, bounded pilots, internal and enterprise workflows, capability exposure, protocol experimentation, data preparation, evaluation, security, and evidence of unit-level value.

### Horizon 2: 18–36 months

The emphasis shifts toward scaled production, cross-enterprise coordination, agent-enabled channels, transactional workflows, operating-model redesign, partner ecosystems, and new monetisation models.

### Horizon 3: 3–7 years

The frontier includes persistent agents, machine-to-machine purchasing, autonomous service consumption, agent marketplaces, dynamic contracting, microtransactions, ecosystem restructuring, and new forms of economic participation.

These horizons are scenarios to test, not predetermined forecasts.

## Counter-thesis and falsification conditions

The thesis would be weakened if the research finds that several of the following conditions persist:

1. Users and organisations remain unwilling to delegate meaningful economic authority to agents.
2. Liability, regulation, security, or dispute-resolution problems make agent execution uneconomic.
3. Protocol fragmentation creates more integration complexity than it removes.
4. Major platforms maintain closed ecosystems that prevent meaningful interoperability.
5. Merchants and service providers resist agent access because it weakens differentiation, customer ownership, or margins.
6. Existing APIs, EDI, workflow automation, and conventional software remain more reliable and economical for most transactions.
7. Agent-driven demand fails to improve conversion, cost, speed, quality, or customer outcomes.
8. Model and orchestration costs remain too high for high-volume or low-value interactions.
9. Basic connectivity becomes standardised, but no new defensible value pools emerge.
10. Agentic commerce remains a feature of incumbent channels rather than becoming a distinct economic layer.

## Strategic implication

The appropriate executive response is neither to assume that autonomous commerce will immediately replace existing channels nor to treat agents as another minor interface trend.

Organisations should build **optionality**: strengthen machine-readable data, modular capabilities, identity and authority controls, observability, evaluation, transaction governance, and ecosystem interoperability while targeting bounded use cases that demonstrate measurable value.

The consulting firm should position itself to answer five recurring client questions:

1. Where will agents alter our industry and value chain?
2. Which business models and control points can we defend?
3. Which use cases should we pursue now, later, or not at all?
4. What capabilities, integrations, controls, and partnerships are required?
5. Where should we invest capital, talent, intellectual property, and ecosystem relationships?
